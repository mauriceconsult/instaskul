In 2015, I was teaching communications at a Ugandan university in Juba when South Sudan's civil war broke out.

One afternoon, walking back from a neighborhood grocer, I was confronted by a group of unarmed juveniles. I wrestled one to the ground and ran. Gunshots echoed everywhere.

The next day, my colleagues smiled sympathetically: **"This is South Sudan."**

A week later, a Kenyan colleague wasn't as lucky. He was killed while praying in his church. No one was prosecuted.

But here's what struck me: **Even amid chaos, students showed up to class.** Despite everything, they wanted to learn.

And we were failing them.

---

## Teaching Across Three Countries

Over 13 years, I taught communications in Nairobi, Kampala, and Juba.

The contrast was shocking:

### Nairobi (2003-2015)
- 200+ graduate students packed into lecture halls
- Needed PA systems to be heard
- Robust EdTech infrastructure
- But still using platforms built for Western contexts

### Kampala (2015-present)
- 15 students around a dining table
- Communications wasn't "popular"
- Limited EdTech adoption
- Tools designed for different realities

### Juba (2015-2016)
- Students choosing journalism were actively discouraged
- Teaching during civil war
- Internet blackouts common
- Western platforms completely inadequate

But the real problem wasn't enrollment.

**It was the tools.**

---

## The Disconnect

Every platform we used was built for:

- ❌ Unlimited internet (we had blackouts)
- ❌ Laptop users (students had phones)
- ❌ Credit cards (we needed mobile money)
- ❌ Western curricula (our context was different)

We were forcing square pegs into round holes.

---

## The Bottom

After leaving South Sudan and returning to Kampala with my young family, I needed work.

My communications degree? Barely marketable.

So I did what you do when your back is against the wall:

**I opened a roadside eatery.** Tea, coffee, snacks.

Doctorate in hand, serving chai to taxi drivers.

That was my bottom.

But it taught me something crucial: **Real African problems need African solutions.** Not adapted Western imports.

---

## Building InstaSkul

16 months ago, I started building what I wish had existed when I was teaching.

A learning management system that actually works for African classrooms:

### ✅ Offline-First
Works during internet blackouts. Download content once, access anywhere.

### ✅ Mobile-Optimized
Built for the phones students actually have, not the laptops they don't.

### ✅ Local Payments
Mobile Money (MTN, Airtel, M-Pesa), not just USD/Visa.

### ✅ Built for Our Context
Designed for African curricula and teaching methodologies.

### ✅ Bandwidth-Efficient
Works on 2G/3G networks. Data is expensive—we respect that.

## Why This Matters

Education is Africa's greatest leverage point.

But we can't build it on tools designed for different realities.

Every time a teacher in Kampala struggles with a platform built for California, we perpetuate dependency.

Every student who can't access learning because "data is expensive" represents unrealized potential.

**I've taught in three countries. Survived a civil war. Run a failed business. Missed my daughter's first steps.**

Every struggle informed what InstaSkul is today.
